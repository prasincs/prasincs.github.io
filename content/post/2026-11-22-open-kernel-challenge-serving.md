*The serving half of the Open Kernel Challenge: making SGLang serve an 816B model faster on a full 8×H200 node, with agents doing the work and one scored run a day.*

> **Disclaimer:** An early draft of this post was generated by Claude from my project notes and records. I edited it from there.

`[TODO: alternative titles: "One scored run a day" / "The judge was the bottleneck" / "2.3× in seven tries".]`

In [the first post]({{< ref "2026-11-20-open-kernel-challenge-agents.md" >}}) I described entering a GPU kernel contest without writing a kernel: a small organization of AI agents did the work, and I made the decisions. That was the kernel track. This post is about the second track, which opened on Nov 12 and closed on Nov 20.

The kernel track timed one layer of the model on one GPU, and you could score ten times a day. The serving track timed the whole model, all 816 billion parameters, served by SGLang on a full node of eight H200 GPUs, to simulated users having long coding conversations. You got **one scored run a day**. Every other piece of feedback came from boxes I rented by the hour.

I finished at **2.315×** stock SGLang, after seven judged runs and about **$685** of GPU time. `[TODO: final rank once the serving leaderboard is public.]` Other teams reported 3.0–3.8× in the contest channel, so this is not a winner's story. It's a story about working with very little feedback: what the agents built to get more of it, where their own measurements misled them, and what one person can do in eight days with a day job.

## The serving track, briefly

You submit a patch to SGLang (v0.5.23, under 400 KB, files under `python/sglang/` only) and a list of launch flags from an allowed set. The judge serves [Muse Spark Open](https://openkernelchallenge.dev) on one 8×H200 node with the contest's settings and plays multi-turn coding conversations at it:

- **The workload.** 13-turn conversations with a 74,160-token first turn, about 750 new tokens per later turn, and exactly 220 output tokens per turn, at 1, 4, 16 and 32 concurrent users.
- **The settings.** Tensor parallelism across the eight GPUs for attention, expert parallelism for the MoE layers, FP8 KV cache, a hierarchical cache that spills to host memory, 16K-token prefill chunks, and speculative decoding with a small drafter (DFlash) that guesses 8 tokens ahead. About 4 of the 8 are accepted per step.
- **The score.** For each load, your throughput per GPU read at stock SGLang's per-user speed, divided by stock's. The score is the geometric mean over the four loads, so each counts equally.
- **The gates.** Later-turn time to first token within 2× stock's; GPU memory within stock + 2 GB; and your answers must agree with stock's, by KL divergence on the logits and by rescoring your turns with stock.
- **The budget.** One scored run and three free checks a day. A check runs one load and doesn't count.
- **The queue.** Runs go in priority order: the kernel track's top 50, then each kernel board's top 10, then the rest of the top 100. I finished that track at #302, so every one of my jobs waited behind all of them.

A few terms come up a lot below:

- **Prefill** is processing the prompt before the first output token. A 74K-token first turn takes seconds, and the user waits for all of it. That wait is the **time to first token (TTFT)**.
- **Decode** is generating output. With speculative decoding, each step verifies the drafter's 8 guesses in one pass of the big model.
- **KV cache** is the per-token memory each conversation keeps so later turns don't recompute the prompt. When it runs out, SGLang moves some of it to host memory and has to load it back.

## Getting eight GPUs at all

For the kernel track, one H200 at a time on [Modal](https://modal.com) was enough. Serving needs eight in one box, a 533 GB gated checkpoint on that box, and the judge's container image. None of that was easy, and the agents spent the first two days on it.

- **The weights were behind a human.** I'd requested access to the gated checkpoint on Oct 25. It sat in manual review for two weeks and was approved the day after the track opened. In the meantime the agents found the same checkpoint on AWS SageMaker JumpStart, accepted its license, and learned that the bytes can only be read from inside SageMaker jobs. They copied it out with a training job whose only work was writing files to our bucket. Then my access came through and we never used the copy. *A license accepted in one product doesn't follow the bytes.*
- **No 8×H200 on demand.** A Modal request for an 8×H200 sat in a queue for 45 minutes without a container. We moved to AWS Spot: p5e in London at about $29 an hour, later p5en in Tokyo. Spot quota existed in only two regions; a request for London was granted the same day.
- **Boxes that boot and can't do anything.** Docker's storage on the Deep Learning AMI ignored its configured data root, and two boxes started, set up their disks, and only then found the token had no access to the weights (about $4.40 for nothing). Every boot script now checks access in its first seconds and uploads its logs even when it stops early.
- **A job loop instead of babysitting.** At $30–42 an hour, idle time is the cost. The agents wrote a small Rust job runner: each box pulls jobs from an S3 queue, uploads results, and terminates itself when the queue is empty. On the busiest night, two boxes spent 87–94% of their lives running jobs, and pulling the 543 GB of weights straight from Hugging Face took 92 seconds.
- **A monitor that couldn't fail.** For nine hours our Spot-capacity report said "no capacity anywhere". The real cause was an expired AWS login, which the scanner read as an empty market. It now fails loudly. *A monitor whose failure looks like a quiet result is worse than none.*

The cost controls got stricter than in the kernel track. I approved every 8×H200 run in chat with an hour cap ("Go, 1.75 h cap"), at most one long packed run a day, and the launcher enforced the cap by terminating the box itself. Short one-GPU runs on Modal were fine any time. After the hang hunt on Nov 14, I paused 8×H200 spend for two days while we waited for the judge's first verdicts.

## Run by run

### p13: the kernels from the kernel track, and a hang only eight GPUs could show

The first patch ported my kernel-track work into SGLang: a fused verify attention and the NVFP4 expert kernel. On one GPU it was fine. On eight, the server hung during startup, while capturing CUDA graphs.

Some bugs exist only at eight GPUs, and the usual tools didn't find this one: host stack traces showed every process waiting, which is what a hang always looks like. What settled it was the GPU's own lightweight core dump, triggered on demand and read offline in cuda-gdb. It showed one block of our MoE kernel spinning at a barrier, because a chunk of tokens with no experts on that GPU read stale shared memory. The fix was two lines. Our own validation run then read 1.33× stock.

The judge read **1.070×**. At 16 users the later-turn latency broke the 2× gate, so that load fell back to stock.

### p15: 13 GB that wasn't where we thought

The judge's report showed our weights using 82.96 GB per GPU against stock's 69.85. The total-memory gate passed, because SGLang sizes the KV cache from whatever memory is free: our extra 13 GB had come straight out of the cache, cutting it from 1.89M to 1.12M tokens. Our own validation logs showed the same 13 GB. Nobody had looked.

The cause took one GPU and about $0.60 to find. Each layer's repacked weights were carved out of a larger temporary buffer that had just been freed, and the rest of that block stayed pinned. Allocating the weights before any temporary existed left zero stranded, with byte-identical weights. **1.104×**, all four loads inside the gates.

### p16: grouped expert GEMMs for prefill

At the judge's 16K prefill chunk, our expert kernel was slower than stock's. For big chunks, p16 decodes four experts at a time into reused scratch memory and runs grouped matrix multiplies, keeping only the packed 4-bit weights resident. **1.218×.**

### p19: someone else had just written the better kernel

The agents went looking upstream for anything on Hopper NVFP4 MoE and found [sglang#50800](https://github.com/sgl-project/sglang/pull/50800), an open pull request with a grouped NVFP4 expert op for Hopper, opened that same day. The research agent first called it unusable for this model. The coordinator read the code instead and found a per-route mode that let stock's own fused post-expert step finish the job.

Before porting it, the agents read the contest rules and terms for anything on third-party code (there was nothing against it) and I approved the port with its notices and credit kept. On one H200 at the model's shapes it ran 2.4–3.3× our own expert kernel at prefill sizes, and it replaced 137 KB of our code. **1.457×.**

### p21: the biggest step was making existing code run on our hardware

SGLang already had a sequence-parallel prefill path for this model, behind a launch flag. Without it, each GPU repeats some of the prefill work for every token; with it, the work is split eight ways. It had no Hopper path. p21 added one, on top of p20 (batching host-cache copies into one transfer per run of slots, our own implementation of an idea AutoclickerI described publicly).

On our rig, a 74K-token prefill went from 6.36 s with stock to 2.74 s. The judge gave **2.208×**, up from 1.457×: more than half of the whole track's gain in one run.

### p24: expert swaps

Each GPU holds 32 of the 256 experts in each layer, and a token's work lands on whichever GPUs hold its experts. Routing traces from a real-weight run showed the busiest GPU doing about 1.5× the average expert work. Eight swaps per layer, fitted on some traces and tested on others, cut that to 1.13×. The organizers had confirmed in the contest channel that rearranging experts was allowed.

Our rig said +3–5%. The judge said **2.222×**, +0.6%. More swaps didn't help: one very hot expert per layer sets the floor.

![Figure 4. Expert-swap balance flattens after eight swaps per layer](/assets/images/okc/serving-swaps.svg)

### p24 + one flag: the KV pool

46 of the model's 62 layers use sliding-window attention with a 2,048-token window, so they need little cache. The other 16 layers attend to everything and need cache for every token of every conversation. SGLang splits the KV memory between the two kinds by a ratio, `--swa-full-tokens-ratio`, that defaults to 0.8. At 32 users, 32 conversations of 75K+ tokens didn't fit in the full-attention share, so later turns spilled to host memory and waited for it to come back.

Setting the ratio to 0.25 regroups the same memory: full-attention capacity went from 2.03M to 3.39M tokens. On our rig, the 32-user load got 7.5% faster and nothing else moved. A free check at 32 users confirmed it on the judge, after waiting 27 hours in the queue. The final scored run, at 00:00 UTC on the last day, read **2.315×**.

## What the score was made of

The organizers didn't publish the scoring formula. The agents reconstructed it from the judge's reports, and it matched p19's four per-load ratios within 2%. Two things fell out of it:

- **Prefill was most of the score.** Speeding up decode raises a load's ratio only through the next load up, so faster decode alone tops out near 3.1× even if it were infinitely fast. To go further you need the first turn: the 74K-token prompt each conversation starts with.
- **At 32 users, the score was memory.** Long conversations that don't fit in the KV cache cost reloads from host memory on every turn.

The two biggest steps were both about the first turn. The time to first token at one user went from 9.3 s to 3.8 s.

![Figure 5. Time to first token for the 74K-token first turn at one user, by run](/assets/images/okc/serving-ttft.svg)

And the 32-user load tracked KV capacity almost exactly, from p13's lost 13 GB to the final flag.

![Figure 6. KV cache capacity and 32-user later-turn latency, by run](/assets/images/okc/serving-kv.svg)

## The judge was the bottleneck

In the kernel track, compute was the limit. Here it was the judge's queue. Outside the priority tiers, my scored runs waited between 2 minutes and 11.5 hours for a node, and free checks waited from 9 hours to more than 2 days. A check filed in the afternoon read back the next morning, sometimes after the patch it was checking had already been replaced. A known-dead check (the p12 hang) couldn't be cancelled; it ran 50 hours later and crashed, as expected.

![Figure 7. How long each judge job waited for a node](/assets/images/okc/serving-queue.svg)

Two habits came out of this:

- **Submit at the reset.** The daily scored run resets at 00:00 UTC. A small script armed the day's choice and sent it at 00:00:30, guarded by the patch's hash so a changed file couldn't go out by accident. The decision lived in a one-line JSON file that the agents could update until a few minutes before the reset, with a fallback if the API refused a flag.
- **The rig had to stand in for the judge.** With a day between verdicts, our own 8×H200 runs were the only fast feedback. That raised the stakes on them measuring the right thing.

## Our rig, against the judge

They often didn't. The rig ranked changes reasonably but sized them badly, in both directions:

![Figure 8. What our rig predicted, and what the judge measured](/assets/images/okc/serving-rig-vs-judge.svg)

- **p13:** the rig said +33%, the judge +7%. The rig's conversations were 4 short turns; the judge's were 13 turns from a 74K-token prompt. Same flags, different workload.
- **p21:** the rig said +71%, the judge +121%. On the rig, most first turns at 4–32 users were prefix-cache hits, so it underweighted prefill, which is exactly where sequence parallelism pays.
- **p24:** the rig said +3–5%, the judge +0.6%. Single passes and changes of a few percent don't survive the trip.
- **Prompt lookup** (drafting from n-grams of the prompt, which the rules allowed): a CPU replay estimated +8–15%. The 8-GPU rig measured +0.5%. It was never judged.
- **The SWA flag:** the rig said +8.2% throughput at 32 users, the judge +6.0%. The first one that carried over at about its size, perhaps because it changes memory capacity, not kernel speed.

After p13, the agents rebuilt the rig's harness to the judge's shape: 13 turns, a 74.7K-token first turn, 220 output tokens, fresh conversations per load. It still sent text to the chat endpoint where the judge sends token IDs, which added about 130 ms of tokenizing to every later-turn latency we measured. A profile caught that on Nov 18.

<details>
<summary><b>What one GPU can and can't tell you</b></summary>

Most experiments didn't need eight GPUs. A "mini engine" on one H200 ran one rank's slice of the model (its 16 attention heads, its 32 experts, the replicated parts) with real dimensions and dummy weights, in the judge's image with the judge's graph settings. It was good for kernel shapes, memory, graph capture, crashes, and "does this switch change the outputs", which is most of a patch's risk, for a few dollars a run.

It couldn't measure anything that depends on real tokens: draft acceptance, prompt-lookup hits, real expert routing. Loading one rank's real weights doesn't fix that, since each layer still lacks the other seven ranks' share.

The idea I'd use next time is record and replay. One real-weight 8×H200 run records the output tokens and each layer's chosen experts. Because the judge decodes greedily, the output doesn't depend on the drafter, so any drafting policy's acceptance can be simulated exactly on a CPU from that trace, and the routing trace can be replayed into the one-GPU rig for expert placement. One team in the channel reported spending about 300 H200-hours; most of the A/B questions hours like those answer could come from one recorded run.

</details>

## Hunting for 3×, and stopping

After p21, I asked the agents for 3×. The coordinator's ceiling analysis said the realistic target was about 2.0–2.1× (we passed it) and that decode work alone couldn't reach the leaders. I asked anyway, then said "go hard for 3x", and approved a four-GPU Modal test and one more 8×H200 run.

What that bought:

- **Overlapping the prefill collectives with the matrix multiplies** on a side stream. It engaged on all eight GPUs and made prefill 1% slower: the collectives queued behind GEMMs that already filled every SM. The next step would have been copy-engine transfers, which need no SMs, and there wasn't time to build and check one.
- **The SWA flag**, the one that shipped.
- **An outside scan.** I had Grok look for levers that could reach 3×, and the agents checked each claim against our pinned SGLang source. Most were real mechanisms that didn't fit: more draft tokens (the drafter was trained for 8, and verifying 16 doubles the work at 32 users), symmetric-memory collectives (their 4 GB preallocation breaks the memory gate), more expert swaps (flat past eight), and an adaptive verify length that SGLang only supports for a different drafter. None was worth the last day's only scored run without a check, and checks took a day.

On the last day I declined a second 8×H200 run and kept the plan. The last slot went to the change that a judge check had already confirmed.

## The setup, one track later

The organization from the kernel track carried over, with roles adjusted for a track that ran on rented 8-GPU boxes and one verdict a day:

| Session | Role |
|---|---|
| OKC7 | Serving lead: patches, one-GPU tests, own-rig runs, the daily choice for the scored slot |
| AWS session | Spot capacity, boxes, weights, the job loop, cost reports |
| Research and writing | Visualizations, the follow-up log, these posts. Read-only on GPUs and the judge |
| Astra (ChatGPT) | Adversarial review of every patch family and every reported number, through the handoff queue |
| Scheduled trackers | A periodic tracker that refreshed spend and wrote the timeline; waiters that armed each day's submit |

A few things worked noticeably better than in the kernel track:

- **The reviewer caught wording as much as bugs.** Astra flagged "bit-equal" where the evidence only showed value-equal (twice), a lever we'd already measured on Nov 13, a one-repetition check described as a match, and a mislabelled latency figure in our own prompts. A model checking its own reports rarely does this.
- **Rules before ports.** Before porting the upstream PR, the agents saved the live rules and the terms and checked for third-party code clauses. I approved per piece, with credit kept.
- **The contest channel was read-only.** Organizers' answers in the channel (greedy decoding, prompt lookup and wider verify allowed, expert swaps allowed) went into a notes file. Nothing was posted.

## Wrong turns

1. **The rig's workload didn't match the judge's.** Same flags, different conversations, and p13's 1.33× became 1.07×. Reading the judge's workload report on day 3 should have been day 1.
2. **Nobody looked at the weight memory.** Our own logs showed +13 GB from the first validation run. A total-memory gate can't see memory that came out of the cache; only the KV capacity line shows it.
3. **Single-pass rig deltas of a few percent.** p24's +3–5% was +0.6% on the judge, and prompt lookup's +8–15% replay was +0.5% on the rig. Below about 5%, one pass on our rig told us the sign at best.
4. **A profiler that never flushed.** The n21 first-turn profiles asked for 8 steps; a prompt is about 5, so the profiler never stopped and we lost the traces from our biggest run. The harness now stops it explicitly.
5. **I started serving late.** The serving notes were parked on Oct 25 while I pushed the kernel track, and the gated weights request sat for two weeks.

## What I learned

- **When feedback is scarce, the proxy is the product.** With one verdict a day, the quality of my own measurements decided more than any single idea did. A rig that ranks right but sizes wrong is still useful, as long as you know which of the two you're asking it.
- **Memory is a score.** The track's cheapest steps were about memory, not kernels: 13 GB given back, and one flag that regrouped the same memory.
- **Read upstream before writing.** The two biggest steps came from code that already existed: an open pull request and a disabled path in SGLang itself.
- **Plan around the worst queue, not the last one.** Arm the submit for the reset, decide the day before, and treat a check as a next-day answer.
- **Put the cap in the launcher, again.** Every 8×H200 run had an hour cap enforced by the box itself. The one overspend of the track was a sandbox created before the stop-all command existed.

## In retrospect

- **Day 1: read the judge's workload and reproduce it exactly**, including token IDs instead of text.
- **Get the weights and a box before the track opens.** Two weeks of waiting on a gated-model approval ran through the whole kernel track.
- **Record one real-weight run, then replay it.** Draft acceptance, prompt lookup and expert placement could all have been answered on a CPU and one GPU.
- **Print KV capacity next to every result.** It explained the 32-user load at every step.
- **Look for disabled paths.** p21's sequence-parallel prefill was in SGLang all along, behind a flag that didn't support our hardware.
